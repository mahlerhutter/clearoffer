What proof to show on a service page when you have no testimonials yet.

For freelancers, consultants and solo operators who are just starting, or starting something new. Short answer: show the work instead of quoting people about the work. A real sample, a worked example or a visible process is proof a visitor can check for themselves. Below: what counts, what never to fake, where proof goes on the page, and a block you can copy.

What proof is for

A visitor reading your page is asking "will this person actually deliver what the page says?" A testimonial answers that second-hand. When you don't have one yet, you can often answer it first-hand, by letting the visitor see what you deliver before they pay.

Rule of thumb: proof a visitor can inspect beats proof they have to believe. A sample they can open is stronger than an anonymous "Great work! – J." quote.

Honest proof you can show from day one

1 · A sample of the deliverable

The exact format the buyer will receive (report, one-pager, design file, audit), made on your own material or on a clearly labelled fictional case. Say plainly that it's a sample. Best for: fixed-scope, productised services.

2 · A worked example

Apply your method to something public or your own (your own website, an open-source project, a fictional brief) and show the steps and the result. Only use another business's material with their permission. Best for: consulting, copywriting, analysis.

3 · Your process, step by step

What happens after payment, what you need from the buyer, what you check, what they get and by when. A specific process signals experience better than adjectives do.

4 · Your own past work you're allowed to show

Projects from a previous job or side projects, described accurately ("built while employed at …", "personal project"). Check confidentiality agreements first, and never imply a former employer's client is your client.

5 · Credentials a visitor can verify

Qualifications, memberships or certifications with a link or registration number. If it can't be checked, it reads as decoration.

6 · Clear, specific terms

A fixed price, a defined deliverable, a delivery time and what happens if you can't deliver (for example, a refund). Specific terms lower the risk of trying someone new. Don't present rights buyers already have by law, such as an EU consumer's statutory withdrawal right, as your special guarantee.

7 · A real person behind the page

Your name, your legal entity, an address and a working email. If you use AI or other people to deliver, say so. Being identifiable is proof of accountability, even before anyone has reviewed you.

What never to fake

  • Invented or AI-generated testimonials, or quotes from friends presented as clients.
  • Logos of companies you haven't worked for as a client, or "as seen in" badges without a real mention.
  • Unbacked numbers such as "trusted by 500+ businesses" or "98% satisfaction" with nothing behind them.
  • Stock photos posed as clients or as your team.
  • Paid or incentivised reviews without saying so.

In the EU, fake reviews and misleading claims about reviews are banned under consumer-protection law (the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive as amended in 2019), and visitors who spot one fake detail tend to distrust the rest of the page.

How to get your first real testimonials

  1. Deliver the first few jobs carefully, at your normal price or a clearly stated introductory price.
  2. After delivery, ask one specific question: "What changed for you after this?" Specific answers are more convincing than general praise.
  3. Ask for permission to quote, and how they want to be named (full name and role, first name only, or company).
  4. Quote them as written. Don't polish the words into marketing copy.
  5. Until then, keep the sample and process as your proof, and don't pad the page with placeholders.

Where proof goes on the page

  • Next to the claim it supports. If you promise a clear report, link the sample right there.
  • Close to the price (see the price section guide), where the visitor decides whether it's worth it.
  • Just before the final call to action (see the call to action guide), as a quieter secondary link such as "See a sample".

One strong, inspectable piece of proof in the right place does more than a wall of vague praise at the bottom.

Copy-paste proof block

Replace the brackets. Delete any line that isn't true for you.

See exactly what you get: [link to sample deliverable] (sample on [own / fictional] material)

How I work: 1) you [pay / book]  2) you send [what]  3) I [do what]  4) you receive [what] by [when]

Who's behind this: [name], [legal entity], [city, country] · [email] · [verifiable credential + link, if any]

If I can't deliver: [what happens, e.g. full refund]

Quick self-check

  • Can a visitor see a sample or worked example before paying?
  • Is every quote, logo and number on the page real and backed by something you could show?
  • Is the process specific: steps, inputs, delivery time?
  • Is it clear who is behind the page and how to reach them?
  • Is the proof placed next to the claim, the price and the final call to action?
