How to say who your service is for on the first screen, so the right buyer thinks "that's me".

For freelancers, consultants and solo operators. Short answer: name your buyer in the headline or the line right under it, using words they would use about themselves: a role, a situation or a problem. "For businesses" or "for anyone who wants to grow" fits everyone and convinces no one. Below: three ways to name the buyer, how narrow to go, what to do with more than one audience, made-up before/after rewrites, common mistakes and a line you can copy.

Why the first screen has to name the buyer

A stranger on your page decides within a few seconds whether to keep reading. The first question in their head is not "is this good?" but "is this for me?". If the first screen doesn't answer that, even a strong offer further down never gets read. Naming the buyer also makes everything else on the page easier to write: the problem, the deliverable and the price all depend on who you are talking to.

Rule of thumb: if you could paste your first screen onto a competitor's site in a different field and it would still make sense, it doesn't name your buyer yet.

Three ways to name who it is for

1 · By role or field

Who they are at work: "for independent physiotherapists", "for wedding photographers", "for two-person accounting firms". Works best when your buyers share a job title or industry and recognise it instantly.

2 · By situation or stage

Where they are right now: "for consultants leaving their agency job", "for shops moving off a marketplace", "for coaches launching their first paid programme". Works when buyers come from different fields but share a moment.

3 · By problem

What is going wrong: "for freelancers whose site gets visits but no enquiries". Works when the problem is specific and the buyer would describe it the same way. (Phrasing the problem in their words is check 3 of the checklist.)

You can combine two: role plus situation ("for physiotherapists opening their own practice") is often the clearest. Three at once usually gets too long for a first screen.

How narrow should you go?

Narrow enough that the right buyer recognises themselves, broad enough that they exist in numbers you can sell to. A few questions help:

  • Have you actually worked with, or talked to, people like this? Name the group you know, not the one that sounds biggest.
  • Would they use this word for themselves? "SMEs" and "solopreneurs" are often your words, not theirs. "Small shop owner" or "freelance designer" may be closer.
  • Can you find them? If you can name a place where they search, gather or read, the group is concrete enough.
  • Does the offer change for them? If the deliverable, examples or price would be the same for any buyer, a narrow label alone won't convince. Adjust the examples on the page too.

On the fear of excluding people: naming one buyer doesn't forbid anyone else from booking. People outside the label who still see the fit will usually write anyway. What a vague first screen loses is the buyers who would have been a perfect fit but didn't recognise themselves.

Before and after (made-up examples)

These are invented to show the pattern. They are not real clients or real pages.

Web designer

Before: "Beautiful websites for businesses of all sizes."
After: "A five-page website for physiotherapy practices, live in three weeks, with online booking built in."

Bookkeeper

Before: "Professional bookkeeping services you can trust."
After: "Monthly bookkeeping for freelancers who dread their tax return. Send receipts by photo, get a clean ledger every month."

Business coach

Before: "Unlock your full potential."
After: "For consultants in their first year after leaving a firm: a six-week programme to land your first three retainer clients."

Copywriter

Before: "Words that work, for brands that matter."
After: "Product descriptions for small online shops selling handmade goods. Twenty products rewritten in five working days."

In each case the "after" names the buyer in their own words and pairs it with the outcome. For the outcome half, see the service page headline guide.

If you serve more than one audience

  • Lead with the main one. Pick the group that brings most of your work or that you want more of, and name it on the first screen.
  • Mention the others lower down, in a short "also for" line or a section with one sentence per group.
  • If the offers really differ, give each audience its own page with its own headline, examples and price. One page per buyer is easier to read than one page for everyone.
  • Don't list five audiences in the headline. "For coaches, consultants, agencies, startups and creators" reads like "for anyone".

Where to put it

  • Headline or the line right under it. Visible without scrolling, also on a phone (check it with the phone test).
  • Repeat it in the page title and meta description, so the search result already says who it is for.
  • Echo it near the button, e.g. "Built for practices with one to five therapists", so the buyer is reminded right before the click.
  • Optionally add a short "not for" line, e.g. "Not for hospitals or clinic chains." It saves both sides time and makes the "for" line more believable.

Common mistakes

  • "For businesses", "for everyone", "for anyone who…": technically true, practically invisible.
  • Jargon labels buyers don't use about themselves ("SMB decision-makers", "knowledge workers").
  • The audience only appears far down the page, in an "about" section nobody reaches.
  • Naming an audience you have no experience with because it sounds lucrative. Buyers notice when the examples don't fit.
  • A narrow label with generic examples: "for dentists" on top, then stock text that could be about any business.

Copy-paste line

Fill it in, then shorten until it fits under your headline on a phone.

For [role or field] who [situation or problem, in their words]:
[outcome] in [timeframe or format].
Not for: [who you don't serve] (optional)

Quick self-check

  • Can a stranger tell who the page is for without scrolling, on a phone?
  • Would your best past client recognise themselves in the words you used?
  • Could the first screen be pasted onto a page in a different field and still make sense? (If yes, it's too vague.)
  • Do the examples further down fit the same buyer?
