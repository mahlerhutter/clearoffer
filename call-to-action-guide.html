One call to action or several? Choosing the buttons on a service page.

For freelancers, consultants and solo operators who sell from one page. Short answer: one primary action, at most one quieter secondary action, and everything else as plain links. Below: how to decide which action is primary, when a second one earns its place, how to word the button, where to put it, and a block you can copy.

Why one primary action

A visitor who has read your offer has one question left: "what do I do now?" Every extra button is another answer they have to weigh. With "Book a call", "Download the brochure", "Subscribe" and "Follow us" side by side, the easiest choice is often none of them.

Rule of thumb: if you had to remove every button but one, which one would you keep? That is your primary action. Make it the only filled, coloured button on the page.

Step 1 · Pick the primary action from how you actually sell

Fixed price, same deliverable every time

Primary: pay or order directly ("Pay €390 and send your brief"). A call adds a step the buyer doesn't need.

Price depends on scope

Primary: book a scoping call or send a short request form ("Book a 20-minute scoping call"). Say what the call is for and that it's free, if it is.

High-ticket or long engagement

Primary: an application or intro call. Being explicit ("We'll tell you on the call if we're not the right fit") lowers the fear of a sales pitch.

Avoid: making "Contact" the primary action when you could name the real next step. "Contact" tells the visitor nothing about what happens after the click.

Step 2 · Decide whether a secondary action earns its place

A second action is useful only for visitors who are interested but not ready yet. It should be clearly lower in commitment and visually quieter (outline button or text link).

  • Good secondary actions: "See a sample", "Read a case", "See how it works", a free checklist that relates to the offer.
  • Weak secondary actions: "Follow us", a general newsletter, "Learn more" that leads to the same page, social icons next to the main button.
  • Never: two actions with the same weight that both cost the visitor something ("Book a call" and "Request a quote" next to each other).

Test: does the secondary action move the visitor closer to the primary one? A sample does. Your Instagram usually doesn't.

Step 3 · Word the button as verb + outcome

  • Weak: "Submit", "Click here", "Learn more", "Get started" (started with what?).
  • Better: "Book the audit", "Get my report", "Pay €390 and send your brief", "Book a 20-minute call".
  • Include money or time when it's known. A button that says what it costs or how long it takes removes the last surprise.
  • Keep the same wording every time the primary action appears, so visitors know it's the same step.

Step 4 · Place it where the decision happens

  • First screen: once, right after the headline and a one-line description of the offer.
  • Next to the price (see the price section guide).
  • At the end of the page, after proof and FAQ, for readers who scrolled all the way down.
  • On mobile: big enough to tap, not hidden behind a menu, and tested on your own phone all the way to the end (form sent, payment page opened).

Repeating the same primary action three times is fine. Adding three different actions is the problem.

Step 5 · Explain the next step before the click

Right under the button, one line on what happens: what they send, when they pay, what they get and when. People hesitate when they can't tell whether a click means paying, filling a form, or getting a sales call.

Common mistakes to fix first

  • Pop-ups before the visitor has read anything (newsletter, chat bubble, discount wheel) competing with the real action.
  • Fake urgency ("only 2 spots left" when there's no limit). In the EU this can breach consumer-protection rules, and visitors notice.
  • A button that leads to a generic contact form asking for budget, company size and phone number when you only need an email and a URL.
  • Different wording for the same step ("Book now", "Schedule", "Let's talk"), so visitors wonder whether they're different things.
  • Header navigation louder than the button, e.g. a coloured "Contact" link in the menu that pulls attention from the main action.

Copy-paste CTA block

Replace the brackets. Delete any line that isn't true for you.

[ Primary button: Verb + outcome, e.g. "Book the audit (€[price])" ]

How it works: 1) you [pay / book / send]  2) you send [what]  3) you receive [what] by [when]

Not ready yet? [See a sample] · [Read how it works]   (plain links, not buttons)

Quick self-check

  • Is there exactly one filled, coloured button style on the page?
  • Does the button say what happens (verb + outcome), not "Submit" or "Learn more"?
  • Does the same action appear in the first screen, next to the price, and at the end?
  • Is the next step explained in one line under the button?
  • Does any secondary action move people toward the primary one?
